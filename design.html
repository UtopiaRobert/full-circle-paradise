The design process

The final map is not the beginning. It is the visible result of a cycle that remains active after implementation.

  1. 01ObserveWalk, listen, photograph and notice what already works.
  2. 02GatherSurvey people, climate, history, water, buildings, soil and ecology.
  3. 03MapPlace fixed features, sectors, zones, flows and microclimates in relation.
  4. 04ImagineTest concepts, functions, redundancies and possible successions.
  5. 05DesignCombine the strongest relationships into a whole-system proposal.
  6. 06Implement & evaluateUse small steps, observe results and revise continuously.

Useful plants in the regional notebook

Five species worth remembering

The workbook's local plant survey connects ecological fit, cultural knowledge, habitat value and practical use.

Wet meadow

Sweetgrass

An aromatic perennial of moist ground. The workbook records Indigenous ceremonial, basketry and traditional herbal uses.

Shady woodland

Hummingbird sage

A fragrant California native with spring flower spikes, suited to shaded oak woodland and chaparral edges.

Coastal sage scrub

White sage

A culturally important Southern California species and a valuable food source for bees, butterflies and birds.

Moist drainage

California mugwort

A rhizomatous native of riparian places, noted for traditional ceremonial use, restoration and erosion control.

Pollinator perennial

Yarrow

A widespread flowering plant with a long record of traditional wound-related and household uses.

Traditional uses are presented as part of the historical workbook record, not as medical advice. Harvesting culturally significant native plants should be ethical, legal and respectful.

The workbook field notes

Place & community

01A community born from rebuilding

The farm began after a fire destroyed the original house. People were invited to help rebuild in exchange for a place to live, creating the seed of a long-lived intentional community.

Workbook pp. 40
02Five and a half acres, many ways of dwelling

The 2022 survey describes a main house, cabins, trailers, motorhomes, a tiny home, earth structures, a workshop, barn, garden shed and other small buildings supporting about twenty residents.

Workbook pp. 40
03Zone 1 is the social heart

The house, workshop and bathhouse form the most frequently crossed part of the land. The design treats human movement and casual encounter as essential infrastructure.

Workbook pp. 58-60
04Two kitchens, shared meals

An indoor vegetarian kitchen and an outdoor meat kitchen served communal cooking. The design also noticed where each kitchen's water went and how those flows could become irrigation.

Workbook pp. 83
05The vision is social as well as ecological

The project vision joins organic farming, self-governance, sovereignty, health, mutual care and education. The farm is conceived as both home and teaching place.

Workbook pp. 53, 87, 103

Climate, land & edges

06A valley site beneath a mountain watershed

The farm sits around 1,250 feet in Upper Ojai, with the Los Padres landscape rising dramatically to the north. That regional setting shapes fire, wind, water and temperature.

Workbook pp. 9
07Mediterranean dryness with long sun seasons

The workbook records about 19 inches of annual rain, 269 sunny days, USDA zone 9b and roughly 13 freezing nights per year: enough warmth for diverse fruit, but with real frost and drought constraints.

Workbook pp. 43-47
08Five distinct microclimates

The design identifies a shaded farm center, sunny humid garden, breezy Persimmon Hill, a hot dry northwest edge and the cool, wet banks of Lion Creek.

Workbook pp. 61-62
09Fire is a design sector, not a background risk

The Thomas Fire burned several smaller structures in 2017. Dense brush, invasive trees and accumulated fuels make phased vegetation management a core safety strategy.

Workbook pp. 55-56
10The boundary must do several jobs

The northern road and nearby neighbors create noise and privacy pressure. Cob walls, planted screens and flowering hedges are proposed as shelter, identity, habitat and enclosure.

Workbook pp. 92-94

Water

11The well is the primary source

The water survey describes a roughly 160-foot well delivering about 20-30 gallons per minute through a two-inch line; the earlier photo caption calls it a 200-foot solar-powered well. The atlas preserves that internal discrepancy rather than choosing a false precision.

Workbook pp. 64, 67-68
12Municipal water is planned redundancy

A high-pressure city line provides backup during summer demand or emergencies, though the community sought to minimize its cost and chlorinated supply.

Workbook pp. 64, 68, 88
13Two large tanks hold emergency capacity

Two 10,000-gallon tanks sit on the hill. The workbook also records serious operational problems: accidental draining, no protective screens and water that was not treated for drinking.

Workbook pp. 15, 88-89
14Rainfall is a vast but brief flow

Using the workbook's site area and annual rainfall, about 2.7 million gallons fall on the property in an average year. A six-inch extreme event was estimated at more than 800,000 gallons.

Workbook pp. 65-66
15Greywater should branch outward

Laundry, sinks, showers and the reverse-osmosis system already returned some water to the land. The design proposes branch drains and selector valves to distribute it deliberately among trees and beds.

Workbook pp. 64-65, 89

Soil & ecology

16The garden soil is overwhelmingly clay

Three jar tests recorded 80-93% clay. That explains slow infiltration, road pooling and the importance of mulch, organic matter and careful earth shaping.

Workbook pp. 76-78
17Percolation varied, but all sites drained slowly

The three garden tests took 25, 30 and 15 minutes. The workbook saw the sites as broadly similar and identified more sand and continued organic amendments as opportunities.

Workbook pp. 78
18Fertility is already produced on site

Kitchen scraps, chipped branches, fallen material and previous crops feed compost, mulch and chop-and-drop systems. The design's task is to organize these flows rather than import every input.

Workbook pp. 78, 85
19The farm participates in a real food web

Gophers and rabbits feed hawks and owls; coyotes and bobcats sometimes prey on chickens. The design must protect production without erasing the predators and habitat around it.

Workbook pp. 69
20Chaparral succession offers a model

The workbook names mustard, horehound, fiddleneck, caterpillar phacelia and deerweed as pioneer plants, and places the site within the California chaparral biome.

Workbook pp. 69-70

Food, animals & vegetation

21The chickens produce a daily yield

The laying flock was recorded at about thirty eggs per day, making the coop a major food-producing element next to the garden.

Workbook pp. 13-14
22Twenty residents create a powerful compost stream

The kitchen was estimated to produce two five-gallon buckets of compostable material each day. Proper bays could convert that steady flow into soil fertility and animal feed.

Workbook pp. 85
23The existing canopy is stored capital

Approximately 330 inventoried plants provide shade, fruit, habitat and climate moderation. The design respects that inheritance even while identifying difficult species.

Workbook pp. 73-74
24Succession must replace functions before removing trees

Arundo, tree-of-heaven, eucalyptus and chinaberry were targeted for phased reduction, while bamboo, sycamore, oak and a mixed useful canopy establish replacement shade, screening and habitat.

Workbook pp. 93-97
25New species were chosen for local performance

The proposal favors Mexicola avocado for frost tolerance, Ceanothus for pollinators and screening, black locust for rapid growth and posts, plus alder, carob, fig, oak and sycamore.

Workbook pp. 94-97

Design & social permaculture

26The ponds become a social landscape

The unfinished pond excavations are envisioned as shaded water bodies with trees, fish and cob seating - water storage, habitat, cooling and gathering in one place.

Workbook pp. 89, 92
27The garden becomes a mandala

A six-pointed garden with a central gazebo reorganizes production around paths, shade, education and beauty rather than treating food growing as a purely utilitarian rectangle.

Workbook pp. 92
28A path reveals the wild edge without domesticating it

A selective trail to Lion Creek would let residents experience the cool riparian corridor while keeping most of it dense and ecologically primary.

Workbook pp. 92, 101
29Governance is part of the site design

Weekly community meetings, conflict mediation, heart-sharing circles and experiments with sociocracy and holacracy show that relationships require design, maintenance and feedback just as water systems do.

Workbook pp. 102
30Redundancy and replication complete the pattern

Multiple water sources, shared tools, compost-chicken-garden loops, repair culture and food storage create resilience. The five-year plan ultimately extends outward through education, a repair café, a time bank and sister farms.

Workbook pp. 101, 104

The gift of the canopy

The established trees create shade, food, wildlife habitat and a cooler human environment. Persimmon, citrus, pomegranate, loquat, olive, apricot and other trees represent decades of stored biological capital.

The management burden

Dense growth also means continual pruning, leaf fall and fire-fuel management. Tree-of-heaven, Chinaberry, giant eucalyptus and invasive creek plants require gradual succession rather than sudden removal.

The next canopy

Replace functions before removing trees

Arundo → bamboo
Exchange an invasive screen for managed, useful plantings.

Eucalyptus → sycamore
Establish tall native canopy before phasing out mature trees.

Tree-of-heaven → mixed canopy
Replace a single aggressive species with oak, support trees and food plants.

Road edge → Ceanothus
Create a flowering privacy screen for butterflies and bees.

Food trees
Add Mexicola avocado, fig, carob and other climate-suited perennial crops.

Wet areas
Use alder, sycamore and riparian vegetation where water supports them.

The test of the design

Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share

The proposal is not complete unless the ecological, human and distributive relationships reinforce one another.

Earth Care

Keep water and fertility in motion

Drought-aware planting, native succession, greywater, rain capture, swales, mulch and perennial systems reduce extraction while rebuilding the land.

People Care

Design for mutual support

Shared meals, social spaces, mediation, accessible paths and decentralized responsibility make community care visible in the physical plan.

Fair Share

Circulate tools, food and surplus

Workshops, vehicles, gardens, cooking, repair knowledge and harvests are treated as shared capacity rather than isolated private assets.

A five-year sequence

Small steps that build capacity

Year 1

Repair and clear

Compost bays, pond work, invasive removal, community-room structure and mobile animal systems.

Year 2

Connect systems

Solar power, greywater branches, garden redesign, shade trees and expanded animal integration.

Year 3

Capture & teach

Rainwater systems, additional compost, more habitat and a public learning garden.

Year 4

Reveal the landscape

Lion Creek trail, restored ponds, a garden gathering place and better Persimmon Hill access.

Year 5

Share outward

Repair café, time bank, public events, work space and sister Full Circle projects.
