Chapter 04 · Slow, spread, store and reuse

Water

Water security depends less on finding a new source than on connecting the sources, tanks, roofs, soils and daily uses that already exist.

Existing sources

The farm’s primary supply is a productive well, with municipal water available as backup. Two large galvanized tanks sit on the northern rise. Their elevation gives them value beyond storage: with the right connections, they can provide water when pumps or power are unavailable.

The problem is fragmented flow

Roofs shed winter rain without consistently capturing it. Laundry and bathing water leave the useful cycle too quickly. Hard clay soil creates surface runoff, while neglected leaks waste high-quality water before it reaches plants.

Core principle
Make each gallon perform more than one useful function before it leaves the site.

Priority sequence

  1. Repair leaks and clearly label controls.
  2. Connect and routinely exercise high-level storage.
  3. Capture roof water near the point of use.
  4. Route suitable greywater to perennial plantings.
  5. Use mulch, basins and contour to hold water in soil.
  6. Keep the creek corridor open enough for safe flow while protecting habitat.

Highest leverage

Begin with the water that already touches a roof or drain

Rainwater

Small distributed tanks reduce the distance water must travel.

Greywater

Branch drains can irrigate perennial trees without complex treatment.

Soil storage

Mulch and organic matter turn the landscape itself into a reservoir.
