The social landscape

A place made by people

The workbook describes a community of roughly twenty residents and three children living among cabins, trailers, motorhomes and shared buildings on 5.5 acres. Full Circle’s history began with people helping rebuild a fire-damaged house in exchange for a place to live, and evolved over decades into an experiment in land stewardship, mutual care and self-governance.

01 · Community history

A community born through rebuilding

Full Circle’s social history began before the 2022 design project. The farm’s founder invited people to help rebuild a house that had been destroyed in an earlier fire, offering a place to live in exchange for their labor. What began as a practical arrangement became a long-running intentional community.

02 · Practices of belonging

Making space for one another

The 2022 workbook records three different meeting forms. Each served a different purpose: governing shared life, resolving conflict, or setting work aside long enough to recognize one another’s gifts.

01

General community meetings

The workbook records Thursday as the regular meeting day. During the period I remember most directly, Jaya ran the general meeting on a biweekly rhythm. The community gathered in a circle, lit a candle, held hands and called in good intentions, placing practical decisions inside a ritual that recalled why everyone had gathered.

02

Conflict resolution

When mediation was needed, meetings followed a nonviolent communication format. The purpose was to hear what mattered beneath a disagreement and seek a workable resolution.

03

Heart-sharing circles

Heart circles were reserved for honoring one another’s gifts. Work and problems were set aside so attention could return to appreciation, relationship and the human beings inside the system.

Social Permaculture Reflections, workbook p. 102

03 · The infrastructure of belonging

Jaya kept the community fed—and meeting

Deanna Jaya Nakosteen was not simply an elder associated with Full Circle. For decades, she operated some of the systems that made communal life possible.

Every day, two residents were assigned to cook a generous vegetarian meal for the community and clean the kitchen afterward. Jaya ran that program: maintaining the schedule, purchasing the food, and keeping the pantry capable of feeding a large household day after day.

Four times a year, bulk deliveries brought the staples of communal life: large bags of rice and lentils, coconut oil, dry goods, spices, toilet paper and other household supplies. The spice inventory became legendary—an unusually rich pantry built for cooks with different tastes and traditions.

Every Sunday, Jaya drove her big yellow car to the Ojai farmers market, often with one person helping her, and returned with a car full of organic produce. She then stopped at Rainbow Bridge, the local organic market, for the remaining items. She repeated this work for years.

She also ran the biweekly general meetings. The food program and the meeting circle were two expressions of the same work: tending the practical structure through which people could live together.

04 · Shared stewardship

Work becomes culture

Planting, mulching, moving materials and maintaining pathways were practical tasks. Done together, they also became ways of teaching, belonging and passing knowledge from one person to another.

05 · Abundance is shared

Harvest as a social rhythm

At Full Circle, food moved through many hands: picked in the orchard, carried to the kitchen, eaten fresh, preserved, dried and placed on a communal table. The harvest was biological abundance made visible—and a recurring reason to work and celebrate together.

“Every week a new fruit ripens. All year long.”

06 · Learning together

Inside the hive

Beekeeping brought observation, patience and collective learning into one small place. A hive inspection became both ecological work and an outdoor classroom.

07 · Ethics made visible

Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share

The workbook’s final narrative explains the three permaculture ethics through ordinary systems already operating on the farm.

Earth Care

Water-conscious land stewardship

Greywater, rainwater harvesting, perennial planting, swales and climate-appropriate design were intended to keep water in living soil and reduce pressure on the well.

People Care

Care beyond formal meetings

The community looked after people when they were sick, helped one another find work and treated mutual aid as part of shared life.

Fair Share

Tools, vehicles, work and food

Residents shared workshop tools, garden tools, occasional vehicles, cooking and harvests. Surplus food was directed toward local shelters or food banks.

Permaculture ethics in practice, workbook pp. 104–105

08 · People in the story

Continuity is carried through relationships

No single person explains Full Circle. Its history belongs to founders, elders, residents, children, visitors and friends whose lives intersected with the land.

Founder

Bob Goddard

Bob founded Full Circle and invited people to help rebuild the original house. The workbook remembers him as fair and accommodating even when he retained final authority.

Matriarch & organizer

Jaya

Jaya ran the shared food program, coordinated bulk provisioning and Sunday produce runs, and held the biweekly meeting rhythm that helped the community function across decades.

The larger community

Many hands, many eras

Residents and guests planted gardens, raised children, cooked meals, repaired buildings, hosted meetings and kept the place alive through constant transition.

A friendship that continued

Alpha

Alpha lived at Full Circle Paradise and became one of my close friends. Our friendship continued beyond the farm: I later visited him at Lost Valley Community in Oregon, we traveled together through Mexico and Guatemala, and he visited me in Portugal in 2024.

That continuing relationship belongs to the social history of Full Circle. Communities are not only places where people live together; they are places where relationships begin and then travel outward.

09 · Governance as an evolving practice

No final form

Full Circle Farm began with a largely patriarchal structure. Bob Goddard, the farm’s owner, held final veto power, although the community still felt democratic and he was remembered as fair and accommodating toward people’s needs.

The community later moved toward sociocracy. By the time I joined, it was changing toward holacracy: a decentralized model in which people lead their own domains and carry responsibility for them.

I appreciated that decentralization. Others found the model confusing or difficult to apply fully, and there was a recurring pull back toward a more familiar central authority.

By 2022, Bob had retired from running the community and Jaya had also offered to step back from its most demanding responsibilities. Full Circle was again in transition, still searching for a durable form of self-governance.

The uncertainty was part of the design story. Social systems, like gardens, are never finished. They require observation, feedback, pruning, patience and the willingness to begin again.

Social Permaculture Reflections, workbook p. 102

My hope was to see holacracy work more fully: a community where leadership was distributed, responsibility was clear, and each person could become the steward of a meaningful domain.

— Social Permaculture Reflections, 2022

Two models in the conversation

Circles, roles and distributed authority

These outside references help explain the governance ideas named in my 2022 reflections. They are not evidence that Full Circle formally adopted either system in full.

Consent & feedback

Sociocracy

Sociocracy organizes work through small circles with defined aims and domains. Policy decisions are made by consent, meeting rounds help every voice enter the conversation, and review dates keep agreements open to feedback and improvement.

Read Sociocracy For All’s overview ↗
One of my favorite ideas

Holacracy

Holacracy replaces a fixed hierarchy of job titles with explicit, evolving roles. It combines clear responsibilities, distributed authority, structured operational meetings and a governance process through which people can propose changes to the organization itself.

What appealed to me was the possibility that leadership could be real without being concentrated: people could hold authority inside clearly defined domains and change those roles as the living system changed.

10 · Proposed social infrastructure

Plans for the next layer of community

The five-year implementation plan imagined social systems as deliberately as water, gardens and buildings. The ideas below are presented as 2022 proposals, not as verified completed projects. Contemporary organizations are included as outside references that help explain what those proposals could have meant in practice.

Historical distinction. Repair Café International, TimeBanks.Org, Make’s Makerspace Directory, Sociocracy For All and Holacracy are reference organizations, not claimed partners or programs of Full Circle Farm.

A workshop as a commons

From “worker space” to makerspace

The workbook proposed a shared worker space. The makerspace movement offers a fuller model: a collaborative workshop where tools, equipment, knowledge and experimentation are held in common. Such a place could support repairs, natural-building prototypes, art, electronics, farm maintenance and small livelihoods without requiring every resident to own every tool.

Explore the makerspace directory ↗
Exchange

Timebank

TimeBanking turns acts of help into reciprocal exchange: one hour given earns one hour that can later be received. Because every hour is counted equally, cooking, transportation, repairs, companionship, teaching and garden work can all become visible contributions to community life.

How TimeBanking works ↗
Repair

Repair Café

A Repair Café is a free gathering place where people bring broken household objects and repair them alongside skilled volunteers. The social value is as important as the repair itself: practical knowledge is passed on, useful things remain in circulation and neighbors learn from one another.

About the Repair Café model ↗
Making

Makerspace

A shared workshop can bring together people from different backgrounds around making, tinkering, creativity and learning. At Full Circle, that could connect existing building knowledge with shared tools, repair events and practical education.

Learning

Public learning garden

A garden designed to invite neighbors and visitors into hands-on permaculture education: propagation, soil care, water conservation, pollinators and perennial food systems learned by doing.

Gathering

Permaculture events

Workshops, harvest days, repair gatherings and seasonal celebrations could connect the farm with the wider Ojai community while allowing residents to teach what they knew.

Replication

Sister farms

The ambitious idea of creating other Full Circle communities based not on copying a fixed blueprint, but on carrying shared ethics into different landscapes and social conditions.
